# DevOps / SRE — Role Overview: The Engineers Who Keep Everything Running

## What DevOps and SRE Actually Mean (and How They Differ)

DevOps and SRE are two distinct labels for roles that overlap significantly in practice. Understanding the difference — and when each label applies — is the first step to navigating this career path effectively in India's job market.

DevOps, as a role title, broadly refers to engineers who build and maintain the infrastructure, pipelines, and tooling that enable software development teams to ship code reliably and quickly. A DevOps engineer sets up the CI/CD pipeline that automatically tests and deploys code, manages the Kubernetes clusters that run the company's applications, provisions cloud infrastructure using tools like Terraform, monitors system health, and responds to infrastructure incidents. The "DevOps" label comes from the philosophy of bridging the traditional gap between "developers" (who write code) and "operations" (who run the servers) — a DevOps engineer does both.

SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) is a specific, more disciplined approach to the same domain, invented at Google and codified in the Google SRE book. SRE frames operational work as a software engineering problem: instead of manually managing servers, you automate everything; instead of reacting to incidents, you define Service Level Objectives (SLOs) and spend engineering effort to meet them; instead of accepting toil (repetitive manual work that could be automated), you eliminate it. In practice, SRE roles tend to be found at larger, more engineering-mature companies — FAANG centres, larger unicorns — while DevOps is the more common title at startups and mid-size companies. The skills required are largely the same; the philosophy and rigor differ.

In India's job market in 2025, DevOps engineers are among the most in-demand technical professionals. Every company that runs software in production needs someone managing infrastructure, deployments, and reliability. The explosive growth of cloud-native architectures — applications built on Kubernetes, managed cloud services, and microservices — has created a massive gap between the demand for DevOps expertise and the supply of engineers who have it. Experienced DevOps engineers with Kubernetes, Terraform, and AWS expertise command compensation packages that rival those of senior backend engineers, and the demand shows no sign of slowing.

The most important mental model for understanding this role: DevOps / SRE engineers are the people who make it possible for everyone else to do their jobs. Without them, developers cannot deploy code confidently, applications fail silently, infrastructure costs spiral out of control, and production incidents take hours to diagnose and fix. The DevOps engineer is the foundation that the entire engineering organization rests on — and the best DevOps engineers treat their platform as a product, with developers as their users.

## The Day-to-Day Across Seniority Levels

The daily work of a DevOps / SRE engineer is uniquely reactive and proactive in a way that distinguishes it from pure software development roles. There are planned deliverables (build this pipeline, migrate this service to Kubernetes, implement this monitoring dashboard) and unplanned work (investigate this production incident, debug this deployment failure, diagnose this sudden spike in latency). The balance between planned and reactive work is a significant source of both challenge and satisfaction in this role.

At the junior level (0–2 years), a DevOps engineer's day involves: implementing pipeline changes and infrastructure improvements under guidance from senior engineers, monitoring dashboards and responding to alerts during on-call shifts, learning the company's tooling and cloud architecture by doing real work on it, and automating manual operational tasks that senior engineers identify. At this level, the most valuable thing you can do is obsessively study the systems you are responsible for — read the architecture documentation, trace every service's dependencies, understand what every metric on every dashboard means and why it matters. The engineers who learn fastest at this stage are the ones who treat every production incident as an education opportunity, not just a problem to resolve.

At the mid-level (2–5 years), the scope expands to owning significant pieces of the infrastructure independently: designing and implementing a new CI/CD pipeline for a team, migrating a set of services from one deployment pattern to another, building the monitoring and alerting infrastructure for a new product area. You are beginning to make architectural decisions — which cloud services to use, how to structure Kubernetes namespaces and RBAC policies, how to design the network topology for a new environment. You also take on-call responsibility independently and are expected to resolve most incidents without escalation.

At the senior level (5+ years), the work shifts to infrastructure strategy and platform engineering. Senior DevOps / SRE engineers define the company's cloud architecture, lead major migrations (monolith to microservices, on-premise to cloud, one cloud provider to another), build the internal developer platform (the tooling and abstractions that make every engineer's development and deployment workflow faster and safer), and establish the operational standards (SLOs, error budgets, incident response processes) for the entire engineering organization. The highest-impact senior DevOps engineers at companies like Swiggy, Razorpay, and PhonePe have often reduced deployment time from hours to minutes, reduced incident mean time to recovery from hours to tens of minutes, and built platforms that make every other engineer in the company more productive.

## The Three Core Responsibilities Every DevOps / SRE Engineer Owns

Regardless of company size, seniority level, or specific tool choices, every DevOps / SRE engineer is responsible for three core outcomes that define the role.

**Deployment velocity** — how quickly and confidently engineering teams can ship code from a developer's laptop to production users. Companies that deploy multiple times per day have a significant competitive advantage over companies that deploy once per week — they can fix bugs faster, respond to user feedback faster, and iterate on product decisions faster. The DevOps engineer is responsible for building and maintaining the CI/CD infrastructure that makes frequent, confident deployments possible: automated testing pipelines that catch regressions before they reach production, deployment processes that are repeatable and auditable, rollback mechanisms that allow bad deploys to be reversed in minutes, and feature flag systems that allow code to be shipped before it is turned on for users.

**System reliability** — whether the applications the company operates remain available and correct for the users who depend on them. SREs define this formally through SLOs: the latency, error rate, and availability commitments that a service makes to its users. Meeting these SLOs requires designing for failure (building systems that degrade gracefully when components fail), implementing observability (logs, metrics, traces that make it possible to diagnose issues quickly), running game days and chaos engineering exercises (deliberately introducing failures to test the system's resilience), and maintaining runbooks (documented response procedures for the most common incidents so that any on-call engineer can respond effectively, not just the expert who built the system).

**Infrastructure efficiency** — ensuring that the company's cloud and infrastructure resources are sized appropriately for the workload and not wasting money on idle capacity. Cloud infrastructure costs are one of the largest and fastest-growing expense lines at Indian product companies. A DevOps engineer who right-sizes Kubernetes resource requests and limits, implements autoscaling policies that add capacity when needed and remove it when demand drops, moves infrequently accessed data to cheaper storage tiers, and purchases Reserved Instances for stable workloads can save a company millions of rupees annually. The ability to translate infrastructure decisions into financial impact is a skill that elevates DevOps engineers to strategic partners in engineering leadership conversations.

# DevOps / SRE Skill Roadmap: From Linux Basics to Production Platform Engineering

## Phase 1 — Linux, Networking, and Scripting Foundations (Month 1–4)

The most common mistake engineers make when starting a DevOps career is jumping straight to tools — Kubernetes, Terraform, Jenkins — before they understand the foundations those tools are built on. Every container runs on a Linux host. Every network communication follows TCP/IP and DNS rules. Every automation script uses shell commands. Engineers who try to use Kubernetes without understanding Linux processes, file systems, and networking spend enormous time debugging problems they cannot explain and fixing configurations they do not understand. Build the foundations first.

**Linux at the system level.** The required depth is not "I know how to use the command line" — it is "I understand how Linux works." The concepts to master: the Linux file system hierarchy (why /etc, /var, /proc, /sys exist and what lives in each), file permissions and ownership (chmod, chown — and what the octal numbers mean), process management (how processes are created with fork/exec, how the kernel schedules them, how to inspect and manage them with ps, top, kill, systemd), the Linux networking stack (how network interfaces work, how routing tables direct traffic, how iptables rules filter packets), disk and storage management (partitions, mount points, inodes, the difference between hard and symbolic links), and log management with journald and the /var/log hierarchy. The resource: "The Linux Command Line" by William Shotts is free online and covers these fundamentals in a practical, readable format. Supplement with Linux Foundation's free introductory courses on edX.

**Shell scripting — automating everything.** Bash scripting is the first automation tool of every DevOps engineer and remains relevant throughout the career. Master: variables and substitution, control flow (if/else, for loops, while loops), functions, reading and writing files, processing command output with pipes and redirects, error handling with exit codes and the set -e option, and writing scripts that are idempotent (can be run multiple times without causing unintended side effects — a critical property for any automation script). After Bash, learn Python at a scripting level — Python is increasingly the preferred language for more complex DevOps automation because it is more readable, testable, and maintainable than Bash for anything longer than 50 lines.

**Networking fundamentals.** Almost every DevOps problem has a networking component. The concepts every DevOps engineer must understand: the TCP/IP model (what happens at each layer when a packet travels from source to destination), DNS (how domain names are resolved to IP addresses, what A records, CNAME records, and MX records mean, how TTL affects caching), HTTP and HTTPS (the request-response cycle, TLS handshake, certificates and certificate authorities), load balancers (how they distribute traffic across multiple backends, the difference between Layer 4 and Layer 7 load balancing), and firewalls and security groups (how inbound and outbound traffic rules work at both the network and host level). A practical exercise: set up a simple web server on a Linux VM, configure a reverse proxy in front of it, add HTTPS with a self-signed certificate, and trace the full network path using traceroute and curl -v. This hands-on exercise teaches more than any tutorial.

## Phase 2 — Containers, Kubernetes, and CI/CD (Month 5–10)

This phase is where you learn the tools that define modern DevOps practice. By the end of this phase, you should be able to containerize an application, deploy it on a Kubernetes cluster, and set up an automated pipeline that deploys it on every code merge.

**Docker — containers from first principles.** Start with why containers exist: the "it works on my machine" problem, environment inconsistency between development and production, and the operational overhead of managing bare-metal servers. A container packages an application with all its dependencies into a single, isolated, portable unit. The concepts to master: writing a Dockerfile (the FROM, RUN, COPY, CMD, and ENTRYPOINT instructions and what each does), building and tagging images, understanding Docker's layer caching (why the order of Dockerfile instructions matters for build performance), running containers with docker run (exposing ports, mounting volumes, setting environment variables), and using Docker Compose to run multi-container applications locally. After these basics, understand the security implications: why running containers as root is dangerous, how to scan images for vulnerabilities, and how Docker's network namespaces isolate container traffic.

**Kubernetes — container orchestration at scale.** Kubernetes is the standard platform for running containerized applications in production, and it is also the most complex tool in the DevOps ecosystem. Approach it in layers. Layer one: understand the architecture (the control plane — API server, etcd, scheduler, controller manager — and the worker nodes that run Pods). Layer two: master the core objects — Pods (the smallest deployable unit), Deployments (managing multiple identical Pods with rolling updates), Services (exposing Pods to network traffic), ConfigMaps and Secrets (injecting configuration), and PersistentVolumes (attaching storage). Layer three: resource management — resource requests and limits (what your application needs and what it is allowed to use), HorizontalPodAutoscaler (scaling based on CPU or custom metrics), and namespaces (logical isolation between teams or environments). The practical resource: "Kubernetes in Action" by Marko Lukša is the most comprehensive beginner-to-intermediate Kubernetes book. The free "Kubernetes the Hard Way" by Kelsey Hightower — building a Kubernetes cluster manually from scratch — teaches the internals better than any other resource.

**CI/CD pipelines — the automation backbone.** A CI/CD (Continuous Integration / Continuous Deployment) pipeline is the automated workflow that takes code from a developer's commit to a running application in production. Every DevOps engineer must be able to build and maintain these pipelines. Learn GitHub Actions (the most widely adopted CI/CD platform in India's startup ecosystem) — understand the workflow YAML syntax, jobs and steps, triggers (push, pull_request, schedule), secrets management, and matrix builds for testing across multiple versions. Also understand Jenkins (common at legacy and enterprise setups), GitLab CI (common at companies using GitLab for source control), and ArgoCD (the GitOps deployment tool that has become the standard for Kubernetes-based deployments). A complete CI/CD pipeline you should be able to build: on every PR, run automated tests, lint, and build the Docker image; on merge to main, push the image to a registry (ECR, Docker Hub), update the Kubernetes manifests, and have ArgoCD detect and apply the change.

## Phase 3 — Infrastructure as Code, Observability, and Cloud Mastery (Month 11–18)

The third phase develops the depth that makes DevOps engineers genuinely strategic: the ability to design, provision, and operate cloud infrastructure at scale using code and data.

**Terraform — infrastructure as code.** Terraform by HashiCorp is the standard for defining cloud infrastructure in code. The core concepts: providers (plugins that interface with cloud APIs — AWS, GCP, Azure), resources (the infrastructure components you define — EC2 instances, S3 buckets, RDS databases, security groups), state (how Terraform tracks what it has created so it can update or destroy it correctly), modules (reusable infrastructure patterns), and workspaces (managing multiple environments — dev, staging, production — from a single Terraform codebase). The essential workflow: write .tf files defining your infrastructure, run terraform plan to see what will be created or changed, run terraform apply to execute those changes, and store the state file in a remote backend (S3 with DynamoDB locking is the standard AWS setup) so the entire team can work from the same state. After mastering Terraform basics, understand the importance of writing modular, DRY Terraform code and how to structure a Terraform repository for a large engineering organization.

**Observability — logs, metrics, and traces.** A system you cannot observe is a system you cannot reliably operate. The three pillars: for logging, understand structured logging (JSON format, consistent fields like service name, trace ID, log level), log aggregation (the ELK stack — Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana — or the newer Grafana Loki, which is lighter and increasingly preferred), and how to write effective log queries to diagnose incidents. For metrics, learn Prometheus (pull-based metrics collection, PromQL query language, alerting rules) and Grafana (building dashboards that visualize Prometheus metrics and make system health immediately legible). For tracing, understand distributed tracing concepts — how a trace ID propagates through multiple services to reconstruct the journey of a single request — and the OpenTelemetry standard for instrumenting applications. The combination of all three enables the goal: when a production incident occurs, you should be able to identify the root cause within minutes, not hours.

# DevOps / SRE Tools and Tech Stack: The Arsenal of Modern Infrastructure Engineering

## Cloud Platforms: AWS, GCP, and Azure in India's Market

AWS (Amazon Web Services) is the dominant cloud platform in India's tech ecosystem. The majority of Indian product companies — from early-stage startups to large unicorns — run their infrastructure on AWS, and AWS expertise is the most consistently demanded skill in DevOps job descriptions across India. The AWS services every DevOps engineer must know deeply: EC2 (virtual machine instances — instance types, AMIs, auto scaling groups, launch templates), VPC (Virtual Private Cloud — subnets, route tables, internet gateways, NAT gateways, security groups, and how to design a secure, multi-tier network architecture), RDS (managed relational databases — multi-AZ deployments for high availability, read replicas for scaling, automated backups and point-in-time recovery), S3 (object storage — bucket policies, versioning, lifecycle rules, storage classes, and CloudFront integration for CDN), EKS (Elastic Kubernetes Service — the managed Kubernetes offering that most AWS-based companies use for container orchestration), ECR (Elastic Container Registry — storing and managing Docker images), Lambda (serverless functions — understanding the execution model, cold starts, and when Lambda is and is not the right choice), and IAM (Identity and Access Management — the security foundation of every AWS account, including roles, policies, and the principle of least privilege).

GCP (Google Cloud Platform) is the second most common cloud platform at Indian companies, particularly at companies building data-intensive or ML-heavy products — GCP's BigQuery and Vertex AI are industry-leading in their respective domains. Azure is common at companies in the enterprise and financial services sectors, particularly those with existing Microsoft relationships.

The certification landscape: AWS certifications are recognized and valued by Indian employers, particularly the AWS Solutions Architect Associate and the AWS DevOps Engineer Professional. These certifications do not substitute for practical experience, but they provide a structured learning path and are a credible signal on a resume, particularly for engineers transitioning from development roles.

## Container and Orchestration Tools

**Docker** is the foundation. Every DevOps engineer must be fluent with Docker: building optimized multi-stage Dockerfiles, understanding image layer caching and how to minimize build times, managing images in a registry, configuring container networking and storage, and understanding container security fundamentals (non-root users, read-only file systems, capability dropping).

**Kubernetes** is the orchestration platform. Beyond the basics covered in the skill roadmap, production Kubernetes expertise includes: Helm (the Kubernetes package manager — writing, maintaining, and deploying Helm charts), RBAC configuration (controlling who can do what in your cluster — a security-critical topic), network policies (controlling which pods can communicate with which — essential for compliance at fintech companies), ingress controllers (routing external traffic to the right services — NGINX Ingress and AWS ALB Ingress Controller are the most common), and cluster autoscaling (automatically adding and removing nodes based on pending pods and current utilization). Kubernetes certifications — the CKA (Certified Kubernetes Administrator) and CKAD (Certified Kubernetes Application Developer) from the Linux Foundation — are highly valued in India's DevOps market and are widely considered the most practically useful certifications in the cloud infrastructure space.

**Istio** and service mesh architectures are becoming increasingly relevant at large companies with complex microservice topologies. A service mesh adds a layer of infrastructure for service-to-service communication — providing mutual TLS (automatic encryption between services), traffic management (canary deployments, circuit breaking, retry policies at the infrastructure level rather than the application level), and distributed tracing without requiring application code changes. Understanding Istio is expected at Senior level at companies with 50+ microservices.

## Observability and Incident Management Stack

The observability stack at most Indian product companies converges on a consistent set of tools, even if the specific configuration varies.

**Prometheus + Grafana** is the standard metrics and dashboarding combination. Prometheus scrapes metrics from instrumented applications and infrastructure components (using exporters for things like PostgreSQL, Redis, and Kubernetes nodes), stores them in its time-series database, and evaluates alerting rules that trigger when metrics cross defined thresholds. Grafana provides the visualization layer — customizable dashboards that make CPU utilization, memory usage, request rates, error rates, and latency percentiles immediately visible. Understanding how to write effective Grafana dashboards — choosing the right visualization type, setting meaningful alert thresholds, and organizing panels so that an on-call engineer can quickly identify the source of an incident — is a practical skill that separates DevOps engineers who are effective during incidents from those who are not.

**ELK Stack or Grafana Loki** for centralized log aggregation. The ELK stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana) has been the standard for years but is resource-intensive and operationally complex. Grafana Loki is a lighter, cheaper alternative that integrates naturally with Prometheus and Grafana and is increasingly preferred at companies that do not need Elasticsearch's full-text search capabilities. Understanding both gives you the context to make the right choice for a given company's scale and budget.

**PagerDuty or Opsgenie** for incident alerting and on-call management. These tools receive alerts from Prometheus (and other sources) and route them to the appropriate on-call engineer via phone call, SMS, and push notification. Understanding escalation policies, on-call rotation schedules, and how to configure alert routing rules is essential operational knowledge.

# DevOps / SRE Learning Path: Resources, Certifications, and Timeline

## Foundation Learning Resources (Month 1–4)

The right resources for the DevOps / SRE foundation phase are those that teach understanding, not just tool usage. The tools change every few years — understanding does not.

**For Linux fundamentals:** "The Linux Command Line" by William Shotts (free at linuxcommand.org) is the primary text. Read it end-to-end, not as a reference. Every chapter has exercises — do them. After Shotts, read relevant chapters of "UNIX and Linux System Administration Handbook" (Nemeth et al.) for depth on topics like networking, storage management, and security. Supplement with the Linux Foundation's "Introduction to Linux" course on edX — free to audit, systematically structured, and globally recognized as good foundational content. Daily practice is non-negotiable: spin up a Linux VM on your laptop using VirtualBox or directly on a cloud provider's free tier (AWS, GCP, and Oracle Cloud all offer free tier VMs), and use it as your daily computing environment for the duration of the foundation phase.

**For networking:** "Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach" by Kurose and Ross is the academic standard — the chapters on the application layer (DNS, HTTP), transport layer (TCP), and network layer (IP, routing) are the most relevant. For practical networking skills in a DevOps context, Julia Evans' networking zines (available at jvns.ca) are short, accurate, and focused on the specific networking knowledge a DevOps engineer uses daily — they are among the most praised educational resources in the DevOps community.

**For scripting:** "The Linux Command Line" covers basic Bash scripting, but for production-quality scripting skills, the "Advanced Bash-Scripting Guide" (tldp.org/LDP/abs/) covers everything you will encounter. For Python automation scripting, "Automate the Boring Stuff with Python" by Al Sweigart (free at automatetheboringstuff.com) is the practical starting point, covering file manipulation, web scraping, API calls, and regular expressions in a DevOps-relevant context.

## Core Tools and Cloud Learning (Month 5–10)

**For Docker:** Docker's official "Get started" tutorial (docs.docker.com/get-started/) is well-maintained and covers the fundamentals correctly. After the official tutorial, "Docker Deep Dive" by Nigel Poulton is a short (200 pages), focused book that provides the conceptual depth the official tutorial lacks. For hands-on practice without a local environment, Play with Docker (labs.play-with-docker.com) provides a free browser-based Docker environment.

**For Kubernetes:** The official Kubernetes documentation (kubernetes.io/docs) is the authoritative source but can be overwhelming as a starting point. Start with "Kubernetes in Action" by Marko Lukša — it provides the conceptual foundation and practical depth that makes the official documentation useful rather than confusing. After the book, work through the official Kubernetes tutorials on Katacoda (an interactive, browser-based lab environment). For the CKA certification specifically, Mumshad Mannambeth's CKA course on Udemy is universally recommended by engineers who have passed the exam — it is the most focused, practical CKA preparation available.

**For Terraform:** HashiCorp's official Terraform tutorials at developer.hashicorp.com/terraform/tutorials are the correct starting point and are genuinely well-written. After the tutorials, "Terraform: Up and Running" by Yevgeniy Brikman is the book that explains how to write production-quality, modular Terraform code — the tutorials show you how Terraform works, Brikman shows you how to use it correctly at scale.

**For AWS:** AWS's own "AWS Cloud Practitioner Essentials" course (free on AWS Skill Builder) provides the broadest introduction. For the Solutions Architect Associate certification (the most commonly recommended first AWS cert), Adrian Cantrill's course is widely regarded as the highest quality and most thorough preparation available — more detailed than Stephane Maarek's popular course but also longer.

## Advanced and Interview Preparation Resources (Month 11–18)

**The SRE books from Google** are foundational texts for the reliability engineering mindset: "Site Reliability Engineering" and "The Site Reliability Workbook" are both free online at sre.google/books. Read them not as tool manuals but as philosophy documents — the concepts of SLOs, error budgets, toil reduction, and blameless postmortems represent a mature operational philosophy that every DevOps engineer should internalize, regardless of whether their company officially practices SRE.

**For incident management and production operations:** "The Practice of Cloud System Administration" by Thomas Limoncelli is the most practical book on production system operations. For observability specifically, "Observability Engineering" by Charity Majors, Liz Fong-Jones, and George Miranda is the definitive resource on building observable systems — it articulates why traditional monitoring is insufficient for distributed systems and how structured observability enables faster incident diagnosis.

**For interview preparation:** DevOps / SRE interviews typically include a combination of Linux and systems knowledge questions, Kubernetes scenario questions, scripting exercises (write a script to do X), CI/CD pipeline design questions, and system design questions with an infrastructure focus. Practice designing infrastructure architectures out loud: "design the AWS infrastructure for a high-traffic e-commerce platform," "design a multi-region deployment architecture for a fintech application." These design exercises are the DevOps equivalent of system design interviews and reward the same preparation approach — understand the patterns, practice the conversation.

# DevOps / SRE Interview Preparation: What to Expect and How to Prepare

## The Structure of DevOps / SRE Interviews in India

DevOps and SRE interviews in India vary more by company than most other engineering roles — because the role itself varies more by company. A DevOps interview at a 10-person startup is primarily about practical skills (can you set up a CI/CD pipeline today?), while an SRE interview at Google Bangalore is about distributed systems thinking, reliability engineering philosophy, and software engineering at the same bar as software engineering roles at Google. Calibrating your preparation to the specific company and role is important.

The most common interview structure at mid-to-large product companies in India: a phone screening with basic Linux and scripting questions, two to three technical rounds covering Linux/systems knowledge, Kubernetes and cloud infrastructure, and CI/CD pipeline design, one infrastructure system design round, and a culture or behavioral interview. SRE roles at FAANG centres add an additional software engineering round — coding in Python or Go at a comparable standard to SDE roles — reflecting the SRE philosophy that reliability work is fundamentally a software engineering discipline.

The preparation timeline for a strong interview performance: four to six months of structured preparation for mid-tier product companies, six to twelve months for FAANG SRE roles. The reason it takes longer than most software engineering interviews is that the breadth of domains (Linux, networking, Kubernetes, cloud, scripting, monitoring, system design) requires more sustained preparation.

## Linux and Systems Knowledge: The Questions That Screen Out Unprepared Candidates

Linux and systems knowledge questions are the first filter in every DevOps interview. These questions test foundational understanding, and there are no shortcuts — either you understand how Linux systems work or you do not.

Common question categories and the depth expected: **Process management** — what happens when you type a command in the terminal? (shell forks a child process, exec replaces the child with the command's binary, the kernel schedules the process); what is the difference between a zombie and an orphan process? (zombie is a terminated process whose exit status has not been collected by its parent; orphan is a process whose parent has terminated and has been re-parented to init/systemd); how do you identify which process is consuming the most CPU? (top, htop, ps aux sorted by CPU).

**File system questions** — what is an inode and what does it contain? (metadata about a file — permissions, ownership, timestamps, pointers to data blocks — but not the file name, which is stored in the directory); what is the difference between a hard link and a symbolic link? (hard link creates another directory entry pointing to the same inode; symbolic link is a separate file containing a path to another file); what happens to a hard-linked file when you delete the original? (the inode is not freed until all hard links are removed).

**Networking questions** — what happens when you type a URL in a browser? (DNS resolution, TCP three-way handshake, HTTP request, server processing, HTTP response, browser rendering — the ability to walk through this in detail is expected at every level); what is the difference between TCP and UDP? (TCP is connection-oriented, reliable, ordered; UDP is connectionless, unreliable, faster — used for DNS, video streaming, gaming); how does NAT work? (translates private IP addresses to a public IP address for outbound traffic, maintaining a translation table to route return traffic correctly).

## Kubernetes Scenario Questions: Testing Operational Depth

Kubernetes scenario questions test whether your knowledge is theoretical or operational — whether you have actually run Kubernetes in production or only read about it.

Common scenarios and the expected response depth: **Debugging a CrashLoopBackOff pod** — what does CrashLoopBackOff mean? (the container is crashing immediately after starting, and Kubernetes is backing off exponentially between restart attempts); how do you diagnose it? (kubectl describe pod to see recent events, kubectl logs --previous to see the logs from the last crash, checking resource limits that might be causing OOMKilled, verifying the container's entrypoint and arguments); what are the common causes? (application crash on startup, missing environment variables or configuration, out-of-memory, failed health checks that kill the container before it is ready).

**Troubleshooting a service not receiving traffic** — the pod is running but the service is not routing traffic to it; how do you diagnose? (verify service selector matches pod labels, verify endpoints object has the pod IP listed, check network policies that might be blocking traffic, test connectivity directly to the pod IP bypassing the service, verify the pod's containerPort matches the service's targetPort).

**Resource management scenarios** — a deployment is evicting pods; why and how do you fix it? (pods without resource requests are assigned to the Burstable or BestEffort QoS class and are the first to be evicted under node pressure; fix by setting appropriate resource requests that reflect actual usage, verified by monitoring historical resource consumption).

## Infrastructure System Design: The DevOps Interview's Most Impactful Round

Infrastructure system design rounds evaluate whether you can design reliable, scalable, secure cloud architectures. The approach is similar to software system design — clarify requirements, work through the design systematically, discuss trade-offs — but the domain is cloud infrastructure rather than software architecture.

Common design prompts and the dimensions they test: **"Design the AWS infrastructure for a high-traffic e-commerce platform"** tests multi-tier architecture (VPC design with public and private subnets, ALB in public subnet, application servers in private subnet, RDS in private subnet), high availability (multi-AZ deployments, auto-scaling groups), content delivery (CloudFront CDN for static assets, S3 for storage), database (RDS Multi-AZ for the transactional database, ElastiCache for session storage and product catalog caching), and security (security groups, WAF, secrets management with AWS Secrets Manager).

**"Design a zero-downtime deployment pipeline"** tests CI/CD knowledge (GitHub Actions, ArgoCD, or Spinnaker), deployment strategies (blue-green vs. canary — the ability to describe each, their trade-offs, and when each is appropriate), Kubernetes rolling update configuration (maxSurge, maxUnavailable), health checks (liveness and readiness probes and why the distinction matters), and rollback mechanisms (how to automatically detect a bad deployment and roll back).

**"Design the observability stack for a microservices application"** tests the full observability picture — structured logging with centralized aggregation, Prometheus metrics with Grafana dashboards, distributed tracing with Jaeger, alerting policies and on-call processes, and SLO definition. The strongest answers include not just the tools but the philosophy: what SLOs would you define, how would you set error budget burn rate alerts, and how would you use the observability data to drive reliability improvements.

# DevOps / SRE Career Progression: From Operator to Platform Architect

## Junior to Mid-Level: Building Operational Credibility (Year 0–3)

The early career of a DevOps / SRE engineer is primarily about building operational credibility — the demonstrated ability to be trusted with the infrastructure that every other engineer depends on. This credibility is built through reliability (systems you touch work more reliably after you touch them), responsiveness (when incidents happen, you engage quickly and communicate clearly), and the visible reduction of operational pain for the development teams around you.

The most accelerating thing a junior DevOps engineer can do is own their on-call rotation completely. When an alert fires and you are on call, own the investigation from start to finish — do not immediately escalate to the senior engineer who built the system. The first few times, you will need guidance. But with each incident you own, you develop the pattern recognition that makes you faster and more confident in subsequent incidents. Engineers who escalate too quickly develop a dependency on senior engineers that limits their growth. Engineers who take the first swing at every incident — even when it takes longer — develop the diagnostic skills that are the core competency of the role.

Document everything you learn. When you debug an issue, write a postmortem. When you figure out how a system works, update the wiki. When you write a new automation script, add comments explaining why each step exists. DevOps knowledge is highly tacit — it lives in the heads of the engineers who built and maintain systems rather than in documentation. Engineers who externalize their knowledge through documentation build a reputation as trusted, organized professionals and simultaneously create leverage: the documentation they write reduces future incidents, reduces onboarding time for new team members, and frees senior engineers from answering the same questions repeatedly.

## Mid-Level to Senior: From Operator to Platform Builder (Year 3–7)

The transition from mid-level to senior DevOps / SRE is characterized by a shift from operating systems to designing systems. A mid-level engineer makes the existing infrastructure work reliably. A senior engineer designs the infrastructure that will need to exist two years from now.

Senior DevOps / SRE engineers build platforms — the shared infrastructure that all engineering teams use to develop, test, deploy, and operate their applications. A developer platform typically includes: a self-service CI/CD system where engineers can deploy their applications without deep Kubernetes expertise, standardized service templates that provide opinionated defaults for logging, monitoring, and health checks out of the box, a secrets management system that makes it easy to inject credentials securely and hard to accidentally expose them, a cost allocation dashboard that makes each team's cloud spend visible, and an internal developer portal (increasingly built on Backstage) that provides a single entry point for all developer tooling.

The platform-as-a-product mindset is the defining characteristic of the best senior DevOps engineers. They treat internal developers as their users, run surveys to understand their pain points, measure the time engineers spend on deployment-related tasks, and set metrics for reducing that time. The impact of this work is often larger than any individual feature shipped — a 30-minute reduction in the deployment pipeline that runs 50 times per day across 20 engineers represents 500 engineer-hours of recovered time per month.

## Staff and Principal SRE: The Systems-Level Thinker (Year 7–15)

Staff and Principal SRE roles exist primarily at large, engineering-mature companies — FAANG centres, large unicorns, companies with 200+ engineers. The scope at this level is the entire engineering organization's operational health, not a specific service or platform.

Staff SRE engineers define the company-wide SLO framework — the standards for how every team defines their reliability commitments, measures their error budgets, and makes data-driven decisions about reliability investment. They lead the adoption of new infrastructure technologies (evaluating eBPF for observability, assessing the trade-offs of migrating from Kafka to AWS Kinesis, evaluating Cilium as a replacement for iptables-based networking). They mentor senior DevOps engineers and model the operational culture — blameless postmortems, error budget policies, chaos engineering practice — that makes the entire engineering organization more reliable.

Principal SRE engineers work at the company-wide or multi-organization level. They make the technology bets that define the company's infrastructure for the next five years, represent the company in the broader DevOps and SRE community (conference talks, open-source contributions, technical blog posts), and advise engineering leadership on the infrastructure implications of business decisions (mergers and acquisitions, geographic expansion, new product lines).

# DevOps / SRE Salary and Market: India and Global Reality in 2025

## The Supply-Demand Gap That Drives Premium Compensation

DevOps / SRE is among the highest-compensating engineering specializations in India at mid-to-senior levels, and the primary driver is the supply-demand gap. Every company running production software needs DevOps expertise, but the pipeline of engineers entering the DevOps career path is significantly smaller than the demand. This imbalance gives experienced DevOps engineers exceptional market power.

The structural reason for the supply shortage: DevOps engineering requires depth across multiple domains simultaneously — Linux systems, networking, cloud infrastructure, container orchestration, scripting, CI/CD, observability — in a way that most other engineering roles do not. A backend engineer can develop deep expertise within the backend domain. A DevOps engineer must be competent across all of the above domains, and true competence requires years of production experience at each layer. Engineers who are strong at Kubernetes but weak on networking, or strong at AWS but weak at scripting, find themselves consistently blocked in production environments where all of these skills interact. The engineers who develop genuine multi-domain competence are a small percentage of the total engineering population, and companies compete intensely for them.

## Compensation at Each Career Stage

At the junior level (0–2 years), DevOps engineers earn ₹6–12 LPA at service companies and ₹12–20 LPA at product companies and startups. The lower end of these ranges is for engineers who are primarily learning on the job — following procedures defined by senior engineers rather than designing them independently. Engineers who have strong Linux and scripting fundamentals and some exposure to Kubernetes at the entry level can command the higher end of the product company range immediately.

At the mid-level (2–5 years), compensation rises sharply. Mid-level DevOps engineers with hands-on Kubernetes, Terraform, and AWS experience earn ₹20–40 LPA at mid-market product companies and ₹35–65 LPA at top-tier product companies. This range reflects the fact that mid-level DevOps engineers who can independently own a Kubernetes cluster, build CI/CD pipelines, and troubleshoot production infrastructure are genuinely scarce.

At the senior level (5+ years), the market is even more competitive. Senior DevOps / SRE engineers with deep cloud architecture expertise, Kubernetes mastery, and a track record of building reliable platforms earn ₹60–120 LPA at top Indian product companies, and ₹80–200 LPA at FAANG centres. The FAANG premium in SRE is particularly large because Google SRE roles are among the most coveted in the industry — the philosophy, the tools, the peer group, and the compensation are all exceptional.

Certifications add measurable compensation value in DevOps / SRE specifically — more so than in most other engineering roles. The CKA (Certified Kubernetes Administrator) adds ₹2–5 LPA to base compensation at most Indian companies. The AWS Solutions Architect Professional or DevOps Engineer Professional adds ₹3–8 LPA. Multiple certifications together can represent a ₹10–15 LPA premium over an otherwise comparable uncertified engineer.

# Transitioning to DevOps / SRE: How to Make the Switch Successfully

## Backend / SDE to DevOps: The Most Common Transition

Software engineers transitioning to DevOps / SRE are the most common source of new DevOps talent at Indian product companies. The transition makes intuitive sense: you already understand software systems, you can read and write code (which makes you dramatically more effective at automation than someone without a software background), and you probably already have opinions about the quality of your deployment pipeline and infrastructure tooling.

The gaps to address are primarily operational and infrastructure-specific. Most software engineers have limited exposure to the full Linux system administration picture — they know enough Linux to use their development environment, but not enough to diagnose network issues, manage disk space intelligently, or understand how the kernel schedules their application's threads. Spend 4–6 weeks specifically on Linux systems depth before focusing on cloud or Kubernetes.

The path that works best for SDE-to-DevOps transitions in practice: start by taking ownership of DevOps-adjacent tasks in your current SDE role. When your team's CI/CD pipeline breaks, be the person who debugs and fixes it. When a new service needs to be Dockerized, volunteer to do it. When the team discusses migrating to Kubernetes, research and present the options. This on-the-job exposure to DevOps work while you are still an SDE builds the practical experience that makes the transition credible to future employers, while simultaneously demonstrating to your current team that you can add value beyond your official role.

After 6–12 months of this on-the-job exposure combined with deliberate self-study, pursue the CKA certification — it signals genuine Kubernetes competence to employers and provides the structured preparation that makes studying efficient. Apply for DevOps roles at companies one tier below your current SDE level — if you are currently an SDE-2, applying for a DevOps SDE-1 role is appropriate because the domain shift represents a genuine experience gap, and it is better to acknowledge that and enter at a level where you can grow than to oversell your experience and find yourself unable to meet expectations.

## System Administrator to DevOps: The Infrastructure-First Transition

System administrators — engineers who have managed on-premise Linux servers, virtualization environments, or legacy infrastructure — have a strong foundation for DevOps work and typically need to add cloud, containers, and automation skills to make the full transition.

The biggest mental shift for sysadmins is from imperative to declarative infrastructure management. A traditional sysadmin logs into a server and runs commands to configure it. A DevOps engineer writes Terraform code that defines the desired state of infrastructure and lets the tool apply it. The first approach is fast for one server; the second approach is the only way to manage hundreds of servers consistently. This paradigm shift — infrastructure as code, immutable infrastructure (servers are replaced, not modified in place), and cattle vs. pets (servers are identical and disposable, not individually configured and irreplaceable) — is the conceptual core of modern DevOps practice.

For sysadmins, the recommended transition path: start with Docker and containerization (which directly replaces VM-based deployment workflows), then Kubernetes, then cloud (AWS is the highest-value first cloud to learn), then Terraform. The scripting skills most sysadmins have (Bash, Python) are directly applicable. The cloud networking knowledge requires building on top of the on-premise networking knowledge you already have — understanding that AWS VPCs, security groups, and route tables are virtual equivalents of physical network infrastructure helps the concepts land faster.

## Your First Week Action Plan in a DevOps / SRE Role

The first week in a DevOps / SRE role is primarily about learning the system — understanding the architecture of what you are responsible for maintaining and operating before you change anything.

Day 1–2: Map every running system. List every service in production, understand what it does and who depends on it, and identify its deployment mechanism (Kubernetes? EC2 auto-scaling group? Lambda?). Draw the dependency graph. This mapping is not just orientation — it is your risk map. The services with the most dependencies and no redundancy are where incidents are most impactful and most likely.

Day 3: Read every runbook and postmortem from the last six months. Postmortems are the most concentrated source of knowledge about how the system fails and how the team responds. The patterns in recent postmortems tell you where the technical debt is, what the common failure modes are, and where monitoring is insufficient.

Day 4–5: Shadow an on-call rotation if one is active. Watch how the senior engineers respond to alerts — what they look at first, which dashboards they open, how they communicate during an incident. This observation teaches more about operational practice than any documentation.

Week 2: Make one small infrastructure improvement — add a missing alert, fix a flaky CI step, improve a runbook. This first change should be low-risk and immediately useful. It establishes the pattern you want to build: every week, the systems you are responsible for should be slightly more reliable, slightly better monitored, and slightly easier to operate than the week before.
